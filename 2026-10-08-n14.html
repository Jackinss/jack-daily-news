The world

Marketing Dive · Oct 5, 2026

TikTok turns the feed into a checkout counter, the same day Advertising Week opens

TikTok shipped a wave of agentic and commerce features: Buy Direct (buying from the feed without leaving the app), a conversational Shopping Assistant that tracks sizing, shipping and preferences, an Agentic Leads tool for DM-based lead capture, Smart+ for search ads, and an expanded GMV Max that automatically shifts budget toward the best-performing TikTok Shop content. It also brought its TikTok Ad Network (TTAN) to the US for the first time, reaching roughly 400,000 third-party apps. According to a company-commissioned report, TikTok generated $81 billion in GDP for US businesses in 2025, with 54 million US consumers saying they bought something after watching a TikTok video.

Why it mattersDiscovery no longer just points elsewhere; TikTok is building the entire sales stack in-house, from first scroll to checkout. The GDP figure is self-reported (a commissioned study), so read it as a negotiating argument aimed at advertisers, not an independent data point.
The Drum, Advertising Week · Oct 5, 2026

Advertising Week New York opens with a creator instead of a traditional ad executive

Dhar Mann, 170 million followers, is the first chief creator officer in Advertising Week's history. He opened the event by launching the "$100 Million Creator Challenge" live on stage, a stated dealmaking target to hit across the event's four days (Oct 5-8, The Penn District, Manhattan), with creators and CMOs (including Hilton, Southwest Airlines, Washington Commanders) pushed to turn conversations into deals on the spot. Mann also teased a 2027 initiative he will reveal later in the week, with no public details yet.

Why it mattersThe hire itself was announced back in June, but today it became a live event: the ad industry is no longer just inviting creators in, it's handing them the main stage and attaching a concrete number ($100 million) to the value it expects them to generate.
National Retail Federation / Prosper Insights & Analytics · survey Sept 1-9, 2026

Halloween 2026 breaks its own record, again

US Halloween spending is forecast to hit $13.5 billion, above last year's record, with average per-person spending at $115.14 ($114.45 in 2025). Candy remains the most popular purchase (96% of consumers, $4.1 billion); adult costumes account for $2 billion, led by witches (4.8 million adults say they plan to dress as one); pet costumes reach $0.92 billion. The survey covered 7,889 consumers, margin of error 1.1 percentage points.

Why it mattersThe holiday is growing in total value without any real increase in per-person spending: the record comes from more people celebrating, not from each person spending more. For anyone working on seasonal activations, the signal is participation volume, not average ticket size.

Campaigns & brand moves

Hypebae, Cosmetics Business · Oct 5, 2026

Sephora Europe "Here It Is"

Sephora Europe's 2026 holiday campaign is built around an original, unreleased song composed by John Legend, "Here It Is," with a 30-second teaser released ahead of the full campaign launch. The mechanism: the product isn't what draws attention to the music, the music is what draws attention to the brand, a reversal of the usual holiday spot with a licensed soundtrack.

Why it mattersIn the extremely saturated holiday ad calendar, owning the musical asset (instead of renting one) is a way to stand out without raising media spend.
Talking Retail · Oct 5, 2026

BrewDog "Choose Craft"

A UK-wide campaign celebrating real craft beer drinker behaviors: keeping empty cans as souvenirs, waiting for the perfect serving temperature, noticing flavor variation between batches. Murals in Manchester and Edinburgh, static and digital ads at major railway and underground stations, and placements near retail stockists. CMO John Beasley describes it as the next step after the recent "Under New Ownership" campaign: from reassurance about the ownership change to pride in the category. No budget or market figures disclosed.

Why it mattersZero promotional or price levers, just recognition of rituals customers already have. It's a cheap way to build category identity when the most dangerous competitor isn't another brand but the generic perception of "beer."
B&T, The Drum, LBB · early October 2026

Liquid Death x goodwipes, phase two

The Liquid Death and goodwipes collaboration on sCREAM Soda flushable wipes (Mountain Water flavor plus aloe and vitamin E) continues, exclusive to Walmart through January 2027. The new video applies the visual codes of luxury skincare advertising, slow motion, glossy macro shots, to a product used to clean up after the bathroom, with the same seriousness you'd use to sell an anti-aging cream.

Why it mattersThe parody works because it respects the grammar of the genre it's mocking down to the last detail, rather than exaggerating it. This is phase two of the same collaboration (phase one, in August, was a sweepstakes to win a dedicated butler): the brand is building a series, not a one-off stunt.

What is working in content

DDAverse, Reviral, industry reports · 2026

The swipe-away window keeps shrinking, and it's platform-specific

The window before a swipe-away keeps narrowing, and it varies by platform: 1.7 seconds on TikTok, 2.1 on Instagram Reels, 2.5 on YouTube Shorts, according to DDAverse's 2026 hook-writing guide (a single agency's benchmark, not a platform-reported figure, flagged as such). In the same vein, Reviral's 2026 short-form data report (also a single source) measures a 22% lift in retention through the first 5 seconds for question-style openings over other hooks, and finds that 86.6% of high-performing short-form videos use word-level animated captions.

On the budget side, TikTok shows the highest short-term ROI for creator campaigns among major platforms, 11.8%, according to an industry influencer marketing report (an aggregate figure, not brand-specific, flagged as such).

Why it mattersThe Liquid Death x goodwipes collaboration (see Campaigns) confirms the same principle from another angle: precisely imitating the codes of a prestige ad genre, applied to a product that doesn't deserve them, remains one of the most reliable ways to stop the scroll.

The historical bridge

1886, North Redwood, Minnesota

A railroad agent sells watches to strangers by mail, and invents a substitute for trust

Richard Sears, a 23-year-old railroad station agent, receives a shipment of gold watches that the local jeweler refuses to accept. Sears buys them himself, resells them by mail to other station agents along the rail line, and nets $5,000 within six months, enough to found the R.W. Sears Watch Company in Minneapolis. The following year he moves to Chicago with partner Alvah Roebuck and publishes the first mail-order catalog of watches and jewelry. The mechanism that makes all of this possible isn't a low price, it's a substitute for trust: before this, you bought a watch from a jeweler you knew in person. Sears proves that a catalog, a written guarantee ("we send it for examination, if you don't like it you pay nothing"), and a flat price can do the same job with a total stranger hundreds of miles away. One hundred and forty years later, TikTok takes the same idea one step further: the written catalog isn't enough anymore, now a conversational assistant answers questions about sizing and shipping in place of a human seller, inside the very feed where you discovered the product.

Why it still holds. Every time the distance between seller and buyer widens, someone invents a substitute for the trust that used to come from a personal relationship: the catalog and the written guarantee in 1886, the TV host in 1986, the AI assistant in 2026. The medium changes, the problem it solves does not.
