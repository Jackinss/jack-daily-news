Roku / advanced-television.com, thedesk.net · November 2, 2026

Roku's ad revenue grows 23% in Q3 2026 even as the price of each ad impression falls 7%, and the company stops giving guidance

Roku reported Q3 2026 total net revenue of $1.5 billion, up 24% year over year, with platform revenue up 23% to $1.3 billion and advertising revenue specifically up 23% to $727.4 million, its ninth straight quarter of double-digit ad growth. The average price Roku earned per video ad impression fell 7% over the same period, so the growth came from more ads shown, not higher prices paid. Adjusted earnings per share came in at $1.20, more than double the $0.53 Wall Street expected. Roku held no earnings call and gave no forward guidance, as it has done since agreeing in June to be acquired by Fox Corporation for $25 billion.

Why it mattersGrowing ad revenue while the price of each ad falls is a volume story, not a pricing-power one, and going quiet on guidance while a buyer waits in the wings is its own kind of signal about who is really steering the business now.

Campaigns & brand moves

John Lewis / Saatchi & Saatchi, retailtimes.co.uk, System1 · November 3, 2026

John Lewis asks shoppers to stop giving cash, and the data says it worked better than the headlines suggest

John Lewis's 2026 Christmas advert, "Gloria and Pickles," is the 20th film in the retailer's franchise and the fourth made by Saatchi & Saatchi since the agency took over the account from adam&eveDDB in 2023. Dame Kristin Scott Thomas plays a grandmother who abandons envelopes of cash for her grandchildren in favor of building them a "Gran Cave" by hand, pushed along by a talking toy dog, Pickles, voiced by Paul Whitehouse, who tells her "you can do better than cash." Sam Fender covers the Rolling Stones' "You Can't Always Get What You Want" for the soundtrack. The tagline is "Don't give up on giving." Online reaction was loud and split: Creative Bloq called it "a mauling," and one Guardian review described it as "a nightmarish allegory for the age of AI," despite the ad containing no AI-generated material. System1's nationally representative testing, which has scored every John Lewis Christmas ad since 2007, rated it 5.6 stars, the brand's strongest Christmas score since "Excitable Edgar" in 2019, with only 6% of viewers registering genuine contempt, about as close as the measure gets to real dislike. Kantar's separate effectiveness read was cooler, finding the ad "lacks punch."

Why it mattersThe cash-to-handmade swap is John Lewis's own version of the argument its critics are making about the ad itself, that the easy, generic choice isn't actually the generous one, whether that's a banknote in an envelope or a one-line pile-on in a comments section.

What is working in content

System1, DesignRush, The Drum, Creative Bloq

Online backlash and measured ad effectiveness are answering two different questions, and John Lewis just proved it again

"Gloria and Pickles" (above) got the kind of coverage that reads like a flop: Creative Bloq called it "a mauling," The Guardian likened it to "a nightmarish allegory for the age of AI." System1's blind, nationally representative testing tells a different story: 5.6 stars, the ad's strongest showing since "Excitable Edgar" in 2019, and only 6% of viewers registering genuine contempt. Kantar's own effectiveness read landed in between, calling the ad memorable but short on "punch," a reminder that even the measurement houses don't fully agree with each other, let alone with social media.

Why it mattersComment sections and measured testing panels are sampling different populations under different incentives. The loudest reactions online skew toward people who already had something to say, while a blind panel captures what an ordinary, nationally representative viewer actually felt; treating the first as a proxy for the second is how brands talk themselves out of ads that are actually working.
Metricool, Socialinsider 2026 benchmarks

Carousels are still quietly out-engaging Reels on Instagram, even as Reels keep winning on reach

Across 2026 platform benchmarks, Instagram carousels average roughly 10-12% more engagement than Reels (0.55% versus 0.52% in one dataset, a wider 10%-versus-6% gap in another), and earn about nine times more saves than single-image posts, per Metricool. Reels still outperform on raw reach and discovery, the entry point for new audiences, but Socialinsider's Q1 2026 data shows Reels engagement softening while carousels have held steady as the format people actually interact with once they arrive.

Why it mattersReach and engagement are different jobs, and chasing one with content built for the other is a common, avoidable way to waste a format: use Reels to get found, carousels to get believed.

The historical bridge

Chicago & New York, 1907-1923

A toothpaste ad run as a controlled experiment, not an opinion, is where "scientific advertising" got its name

In 1907, Albert Lasker hired copywriter Claude Hopkins at Lord & Thomas on an eventual salary of $185,000 a year specifically because Hopkins refused to settle advertising arguments by opinion. Hopkins' method, laid out in his 1923 book "Scientific Advertising," was to run two or three versions of a coupon ad in similar-sized towns and count the actual redemptions, turning "which headline is better" from a boardroom argument into a number anyone could check. His most famous application was Pepsodent toothpaste, built from a single testable claim, that it dissolved the invisible film on teeth, repeated and measured until it became one of the bestselling brands in America. Hopkins put his own philosophy plainly: "Almost any question can be answered cheaply, quickly and finally by a test campaign," not by "arguments around a table," but by going to "the court of last resort, the buyers of your product."

Why it still holds. The same divide that separated Hopkins' coupon counts from boardroom opinion in 1923 is the one separating Creative Bloq's take on John Lewis's 2026 ad from System1's blind panel score today. A century later, it is still cheaper and more honest to test than to argue.
